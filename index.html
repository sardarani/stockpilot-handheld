Summary

The approval is Ravi’s job. Meena’s job is to keep counting.

Meena scans bin 14-C-03. The system says 120 units of gloves; she counts 96. That’s 20% off, past the 10% rule, so it needs a reason and a supervisor’s approval. The risk in that moment runs both ways: a wrong count gets approved, or she stands still waiting while 40 bins go uncounted.

Accuracy

Count blind, confirm once

The expected number stays hidden until she has counted. A gap over 10% suggests one recount and shows the likely cause.

Speed

Send it and walk on

Approval runs in the background. She holds to send, moves to the next bin, and gets a buzz when Ravi decides.

Environment

Built for gloves and glare

High-contrast type, big numbers, 56px pill buttons, no typing anywhere, and vibration plus the scanner’s LED in place of sound.

The floor

Every detail of the scenario became a design rule.

The brief says deciding what matters is part of the task. These are the details I designed around, and what each one changed.

What’s true on the floorWhat it meansWhat I did
She’s wearing glovesFine taps and typing are gone.56px pill buttons, 52px keys, reason rows where the whole row is the target, and the yellow side buttons to scan. No text field anywhere in the flow.
A box in one handOne thumb, and the occasional accidental bump.Every primary action sits at the bottom, in thumb reach. The one action that commits a change, sending for approval, is hold-to-send so a bump can’t trigger it.
The warehouse is noisySounds get lost.Vibration patterns (one pulse for a match, three for a gap) and the scanner’s LED: green for a read, amber for a gap, red for a failed scan.
Lighting variesGlare in bright aisles, low contrast in dim ones.Near-black on white for about 20:1 contrast, 56–64px numerals, bold sentence-case type. Blue for the one action, red only for a gap that needs attention. The light sensor flips to a dark theme in dim aisles.
Gaps over 10% need approvalA rule, not a conversation.The allowed gap is shown in units (±12), not just as a percentage. Under 10%, the count saves with no extra screens.
40 more bins before shift endEvery second on this bin is taken from the next one.A matching bin takes 5 taps. Approval never blocks her. The next bin is on screen the moment she sends.

Left out on purpose: stock value, bin capacity, SKU history and movement logs. None of them help Meena decide anything in the moment, and every extra line competes with the number that matters.

The idea

Add accuracy where it’s cheap, protect speed where it’s expensive, and never make Meena wait for someone else’s decision.

The screens

Four screens and one state. Try them.

Count 96 to see the mismatch, 114 to see a small gap save itself, or 120 for a match. Use the side menu to jump to any screen, or switch to All screens to see them side by side.

Live prototypeOpen full screen
  • Before · Scanning“Scan bin label” or a yellow side button fires the laser. The screen shows the label to aim at; a green LED and a beep confirm the read.
  • 1 · Blind countThe expected quantity is hidden. She can count in cartons of 12, and the button repeats her number back: “Confirm 96 units”.
  • 2 · MismatchThe gap is the headline, “24 units short”, in the only red on screen. Both counts and the allowed gap sit beneath, with the likely cause.
  • 3 · ReasonFour reasons in a list, no typing. The first carries the evidence that matches the gap.
  • 4 · Send for approvalHold to send and go to the next bin. If Ravi is standing there, his PIN on her scanner settles it on the spot.
  • Additional state · Scan failedPlain cause and fix. The fallback is the 2 check digits on the label, which prove she’s at the right bin; a typed bin code wouldn’t.

UX copy

On a 6-inch screen, the words are most of the interface.

Every string was written to answer “what happens if I tap this?” or “what do I do now?”. The rules: say the number, say what happens next, use her words, never blame her.

Decisions

What I chose, and what I turned down.

DecisionRejectedWhy
Blind countShow the expected number next to the inputIt anchors the count. The number appears only after she confirms hers.
Recommend one recountForce a recount on every gapForcing it punishes correct counts. Recommending it catches most miscounts, and the recount is blind too.
Background approvalA waiting screen until Ravi respondsWaiting is the real cost of a mismatch. The bin stays flagged, she gets a buzz, and Undo covers mistakes.
Hold to sendA tap, or slide-to-confirmA tap is easy to bump. A slide needs a steady drag and a free hand. A hold works with one gloved thumb.
A list of reasons with evidenceA dropdown, or a text boxDropdowns are tiny and typing in gloves is slow. Fixed reasons also give Ravi data he can compare.
Check digits as the scan fallbackType the bin codeTyping “14-C-03” is slow and lets her count the wrong bin. Two digits printed on the label prove where she is.
Blue for action, red only for the gapColour on every statusBlue marks the one thing to press on each screen. Red is kept for the gap alone and paired with neutral words (“24 units short”), so it reads as “look here”, not as her mistake. Green only confirms a match or a scan.

Design system

Why Uber’s design language, and what I changed.

I compared four systems from getdesign.md against Meena’s conditions. Uber’s fits best because its own product is used the same way: glanced at on the move, in harsh sun or at night, by someone in the middle of a job.

SystemVerdictReason
UberChosenBlack and white for maximum contrast, bold sentence-case headings, pill buttons on every action, flat surfaces. Built for glancing.
AppleClose secondCalm and familiar, but made for editorial reading: lighter headlines and a blue link style that fades in glare.
CoinbaseTried firstClean, but weight-400 headlines and grey body text lose contrast at arm’s length.
ShopifyRuled outCinematic black-on-black with thin display type, which fails in glare.

Floor adaptations

  • Colour added with a job. Uber is black and white. I added a blue primary for the one action per screen, red only for a gap that needs attention, and green only for a confirmed match.
  • Bigger targets. Uber’s 44px buttons grow to 56px for gloves, keys to 52px.
  • Readable disabled buttons. The instruction (“Enter a count”) stays legible in glare.

Patterns adapted from 15 component libraries

Rebuilt in plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript rather than installed, so they run on a low-end scanner. The patterns and motion come from these libraries; the code is written for this device.

Looked at and left out: WebGL shaders, liquid glass, 3D carousels and scratch cards (awesmos, lightswind, useplanes, spaceui), and slide-to-confirm (spaceui, bencho). A rugged scanner has a weak GPU, a glare-filled screen and a battery that must last a shift. Effects cost all three and don’t help Meena count. Designeer, ovio and prompt-motion were motion references only. Not Quite Unicorns makes Bubble components, which don’t apply to a coded build.

Assumptions and next steps

What I assumed, and how I’d know it works.

Assumptions

  • Units are pairs; gloves come in cartons of 12.
  • Each bin holds one form of stock, either full cartons or loose units. A bin with both is out of scope; the counter enters the total in units.
  • The 10% rule compares against the system quantity, in either direction. Within 10%, the count saves without approval.
  • Supervisors can approve from their own device; a PIN covers approval in person.
  • The warehouse system knows about unconfirmed picks for each bin.
  • The device is a rugged Android scanner (360 × 740) with yellow scan triggers. System UI such as bottom sheets and snackbars follows Android’s Material 3.

How I’d measure it

  • Time per bin, split by matching and mismatched bins.
  • Recount resolution rate: the share of big gaps that a recount clears.
  • Idle time after sending, which should be close to zero.
  • Approved adjustments that hold up in later spot checks.
  • Undo rate, as a signal of accidental sends.

With two more weeks

Test target sizes and hold duration with associates in real gloves. Design the offline queue for dead zones, the wrong-item-in-bin state, and batch approvals for supervisors. Localise for a multilingual workforce.
